# Policies and support

## Everyone sits a different exam. Here is how that is fair.

[Assessment 3](/assessments/03-the-run/) has you pick a subject you dislike and
sit an exam we set on the quarter of it you chose. Your exam is therefore
harder or easier than the person next to you, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest. We considered four ways to handle it.

**Everyone sits the same exam**, on one subject the course nominates. Rejected,
because it destroys the premise. The subject has to be one *you* have already
failed to start, and aversion is not uniform — a subject chosen centrally is
merely unfamiliar to most of the room, which is a much easier problem and not
the one this course is about.

**Scale the scores by subject difficulty.** Rejected. It requires a difficulty
model across arbitrary subjects that we do not have and could not defend if
challenged, and an indefensible model is worse than an acknowledged limitation.

**Mark the raw score.** Rejected outright. It pays you to pick something easy,
and by week 10 you would all have found that out.

**What we do:** the exam is a *criterion*, not a score. It carries 40% and it is
marked against the baseline you wrote in week 10 — the question is whether
genuine movement happened, not how many marks it was worth. Above that bar the
remaining marks go to whether the mechanism caused the movement and whether you
can evidence it, which is the thing this course actually taught.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: **you cannot improve your mark by
picking an easy subject, and you cannot lose marks by picking a brutal one.**
You can lose marks by picking one you secretly quite like, because the evidence
of dislike is marked too.

## Playing games for credit

Two of the twelve weeks are spent playing, and this raises real questions that
a normal course does not have to answer.

**You never have to buy anything.** The School licenses copies for both
[Labs](/sessions/) and the machines are provided. If you would rather use your
own account and your own save, that is fine and slightly better, but it is not
expected and it earns nothing extra.

**You do not have to be good.** Nothing in this course is marked on how you
played. Assessment 1 and 2 are marked on the mechanism you built afterwards.
Arriving at Lab 1 having never queued a match is an ordinary starting point and
there is a beginners' queue for exactly that.

**If you cannot play, tell us in week 1.** Photosensitivity, motion sickness,
RSI, a hand injury, or a considered objection to the content all count, and none
of them requires a diagnosis. The observations the Labs produce can be gathered
by watching someone else play with the note-taking protocol running, or from
recorded play with the same protocol. This is a genuine substitute, not a
consolation one — the notes are the deliverable and the notes are what gets
used.

**Time spent playing is coursework time**, and it is inside the ordinary 130
hours, not on top of them. If a Lab is running you over your budget for the
week, stop playing and come to the tutorial with fewer matches. Eight is a
minimum for a reason and six with good notes beats twelve with none.

## What this course is not

It is a course about reward structures. It is **not** counselling, and the staff
teaching it are not qualified to provide any.

Some of the material — learned helplessness, the evening that ends with nothing
to point at, the difference between resting and not-working — sits close to
things that can be symptoms rather than habits. If working through it starts to
feel like more than a study problem, that is worth taking to someone who is
trained for it. Ask any of [the teaching team](/people/) and we will point you
at the right service; nothing you say to us about it affects your marks or goes
into your record.

Nothing in this course is designed to make you work more hours. Several parts of
it are designed to make the hours you already work produce something, which is a
different goal and occasionally the opposite one.

## Assessment mechanics

**Late work.** The two demonstrations happen live in the lecture slot and cannot
be handed in late; if you cannot attend, arrange an alternative slot beforehand.
Assessment 3's written material is accepted up to five working days late at 5%
per working day, and the exam is fixed.

**Extensions** are granted readily and must be asked for *before* the due date.
The demonstrations can move to another week. Assessment 3's run cannot be
extended past the end of semester, so an extension there moves the written
account, not the thirty hours.

**Marks and feedback** are returned within two weeks. Both demonstrations get
verbal feedback on the day.

## Using AI

Expected, and used throughout — the built route for both mechanisms explicitly
invites it, and building a working tool in an afternoon is a normal way to do
Assessment 1 or 2.

Two limits, and they are about what is being marked rather than about tools.

You must be able to **drive what you submit**. In a demonstration you will be
asked to change something small in front of us. Not being able to is the same
problem whether the thing was generated, copied from a friend, or written by
you in a hurry a month ago.

**The logs must be real.** Everything else in this course can be assisted;
your own record of your own sittings cannot be, because a fabricated log makes
the entire submission meaningless rather than merely dishonest. The failures in
a real log are the most valuable thing in it, and a run with no bad fortnight in
it is not a run we believe.

## Academic integrity

The mechanism you build is yours, and you may borrow freely from published
designs — the reference ladder read in [week 5](/lectures/week-05/) exists to be
copied from. Say where a design came from.

Collaboration on mechanisms is encouraged and collaboration on logs is not,
for the reason above.

## Getting help

Email any of [the teaching team](/people/). Questions about a brief are better
asked in the lecture, where everyone hears the answer.
