Second half is the second assessment. First half is the argument the whole
course has been walking towards, so I want to put it plainly before we get to it.

## Two mechanisms, two jobs

You have now built both. It is worth being exact about what each one is for,
because they are not two flavours of the same thing and using one where you
needed the other is the most common way this all falls apart.

**The ladder buys you the inside of thirty minutes.** Absolute focus and fast
feedback while you are sitting there. It is extremely good at this and nothing
else does it as well.

What does it offer over a semester? Honestly: encouragement. If you climb
quickly and end up at a high rank, you acquire a plain, unadorned confidence —
the kind that comes from a number you trust going up, which is not nothing and
is not a plan. **The ladder cannot break a large task into small ones.** It
never could. Rating systems rank performances; they have no opinion about what
you should do next, and no amount of tuning will give them one.

**Decomposition lives in the Souls half.** Before you start, a Souls game looks
like an unmanageable amount of content — dozens of hours, an unknown number of
areas, no visible end. You finish it anyway, and not through stamina. You finish
it because it is a sequence of bosses, and each individual boss is a thing that
*ends*. The mountain was never climbed; it was resolved into nine fights, and
nine is a number a person can hold.

That capacity — turning an amount of work you cannot face into a list of things
that each terminate — is the single most valuable skill in this course, and it
is the one the ladder was structurally incapable of teaching you.

## What to build

A boss tree, and the honest version has three properties.

**It rolls up.** A boss's remaining work includes its children's. If you mark a
parent done while its children are open, the tree is lying to you, and it will
lie in the flattering direction.

**It survives subdivision.** Halfway through, a boss will turn out to be three
bosses. That is normal and is not failure; it is the fight becoming legible. If
splitting a boss corrupts your history or resets your progress, you will avoid
splitting, and you will keep a wrong plan because the tool made the right one
expensive.

**Nothing has a length you fixed in advance.** This is the one I would fight
for. Estimate the work, and let the duration fall out of the estimate — a
campaign's length is a *forecast that moves when the load moves*, never a
commitment you made at the start. Fix the length up front and you have not set
yourself a campaign, you have sentenced yourself to a term, and the difference
is the whole reason one of them is bearable.

## The unit that finally holds a result

There is a gap between the two mechanisms and it is worth naming, because it is
where most people's motivation actually dies.

A sitting is thirty minutes. A boss might be a hundred hours. A *day* is the
unit we all reach for, and a day is too short to contain a result worth feeling
anything about — which is why so many evenings end with nothing to point at and
the vague sense of having wasted one.

The unit that works is **several days**. Long enough that something finishes
inside it, short enough that you can see the far end from where you are
standing. Pick a handful of bosses, declare them the current push, and do not
put a date on it. When they are done, close it and start another. A few days is
enough time to become noticeably different at something, and that is the
feeling you have been missing.
