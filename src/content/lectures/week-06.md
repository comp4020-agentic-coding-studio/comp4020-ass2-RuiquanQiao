Here is a fact about the games industry that ought to be strange.

Souls games are, by reputation and largely in truth, the least accommodating
mainstream games there are. They kill you constantly, explain almost nothing,
and take the better part of a hundred hours. And a very large number of the
people who finish one are ordinary players who previously bounced off fighting
games — which are, by any mechanical measure, the easier thing to get into. A
fighting game match is three minutes. You can play one badly and still have fun.

So the puzzle is not "why do hard games sell". It is: **why does the harder one
retain the player the easier one lost?**

The answer has nothing to do with difficulty, and you should try to make the
argument this lecture makes without using the word.

## Measure the loop, in two numbers

For any activity, take:

- **Time to failure** — how long from starting an attempt to finding out it
  didn't work.
- **Time to retry** — how long from that until you can try again.

A Souls boss: ninety seconds and fifteen seconds. You die a minute and a half
in, you are back at the fog gate a quarter of a minute later.

A fighting game combo: you go into practice mode and drill a sequence whose real
payoff arrives in a match several sessions from now. Time to failure is short;
time to *finding out whether it mattered* is days.

An assignment: three weeks to failure, and no retry at all.

The Souls loop is short. That is the whole trick, and it is disguised as
cruelty.

## The size of the lesson

A ninety-second attempt is short enough that you only retain one or two things
from it. *It always sweeps after the second swing.* That is a small, cheap,
completely testable hypothesis, and the interesting part is what it does to you:
you want to know **right now** whether that one thing is enough.

It usually isn't. So you learn a second thing. Twenty attempts later you have
twenty small true statements about this boss and you win, and at no point did
you have to decide to be disciplined.

Curiosity carried you, and curiosity is much cheaper than discipline. This is
the design principle the whole second half of the course is built on: **make the
lesson per attempt small enough that testing it is the obvious next move.**

## Two supporting conditions

**Legible tells.** The boss telegraphs. You lost because of something you can
name, which is what makes the lesson extractable. A game that killed you for
reasons you cannot reconstruct produces no hypotheses, and it is the failure to
be legible — not the failure to be fair — that makes people quit.

**The boss does not change.** Between attempt four and attempt twenty the only
variable that moved is you. That is a rare and precious property, and most of
your studying does not have it.

## Before the Lab

Have something installed and ready. Any of the Souls-likes work; you want a game
with discrete boss encounters and a short walk back, not an open-world game you
can wander away from when a fight goes badly. Wandering away is exactly the
behaviour we are studying, and this week you need to not have the option.
