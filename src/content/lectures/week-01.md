"Gamify your studies" almost always means points stapled to the outside of work
that has not changed. You still sit down to the same undifferentiated three
hours; there is just now a streak counter judging you afterwards. It does not
work, and the reason it does not work is worth naming precisely: the reward is
*about* the work rather than *inside* it.

A game does not reward you for having played. It rewards you inside the playing,
continuously, on rules you could recite.

## The six families

- **Score.** Arcade lineage. A number goes up as you act. Needs a machine that
  can tell, instantly and without argument, whether the thing you just did was
  good.
- **The ranked ladder.** MOBA and shooter lineage. Many short contests, a rating
  that survives between them. Needs a win condition per contest and a rating
  rule you trust.
- **Boss gates.** Souls and Metroidvania lineage. Progress is a sequence of
  discrete, defeatable obstacles. Needs the obstacles to be nameable in advance.
- **Collection.** Achievement hunting, completionism. Needs somebody to have
  authored the list.
- **Streaks and dailies.** Free-to-play mobile lineage. Needs a threat — the
  streak's whole power is that it can be broken.
- **Execution mastery.** Fighting games, rhythm games. Needs a long apprenticeship
  before the first payoff, and therefore needs you to already love it.

## Which two survive

Four of these die on contact with your situation.

Score needs an instant impartial judge, and for most study there isn't one.
Collection needs an author, and if you write the list yourself you will write
one you can finish. Streaks work, but they work by threat, and a mechanism that
punishes you for a bad week will be abandoned in the first bad week — which is
exactly the week you needed it. Execution mastery is the trap this course exists
to avoid: it is the model that requires you to be the kind of person who
already puts in the hours.

That leaves the **ranked ladder** and the **boss gate**. The rest of the
semester is those two, in that order, and the last three weeks are you running
both at once on something you actively dislike.

## Three systems that failed, and the number that ended them

I will spend some of this lecture on my own record, because it is the only
evidence I have that the obvious approaches do not work.

I tried an *effort* currency — a point per hard idea got through — because an
hour means different things on different days. It inflated. By the fourth month
things that had not been worth a point were worth one, my daily totals were
climbing, and my output was flat.

I tried decomposition, and got genuinely better at seeing what a problem was
made of, and no better at doing any of it.

Then I tried timing everything, honestly, for nine months. Sixteen waking hours,
six at the desk on a good day. I had guessed four, so the number was better than
I feared, and it still changed nothing — because a timer measures how long you
sat there.

All three measured what went in. That is the comfortable half, and it is the
half that cannot tell you whether the semester is going well.

## Before the lecture

Work out, roughly, the feedback loop in one course you are taking right now: how
many hours of your work pass between doing something and finding out whether it
was any good. Bring the number in hours. Most people's first honest answer is
somewhere between forty and the whole semester.
